Chapter 1 • Geography

An archipelago shaped by mountains, sea and scarce flat land.

Japan has four main islands — Honshu, Hokkaido, Kyushu and Shikoku — and is a mountainous island chain in Eastern Asia. Limited flat land made farming land valuable and helps explain why control of territory mattered so much.

Landscape clue: Farming had to fit around mountains and limited flat land. Rice agriculture became economically and socially important.

City focus

Kyoto / Heian-kyo

Imperial capital • 794–1868

Why it mattered

Kyoto was the home of the emperor and court. It remained the symbolic heart of Japan even when shoguns ruled from other cities.

Historical role

From the Heian period onward, Kyoto shaped literature, religion, architecture and court ceremony. Warrior governments often ruled in the emperor’s name, so Kyoto remained politically important even when military power shifted elsewhere.

City focus

Nara

Imperial capital • 710–794

Why it mattered

Nara was Japan’s first permanent capital and a major centre of Buddhism and early state formation.

Historical role

Nara was planned on Chinese lines and became a centre of temples, learning and government. It helps explain the development of stronger central authority before warrior government.

City focus

Osaka

Castle town & commercial centre

Why it mattered

Osaka became vital during the age of unification and later one of Japan’s busiest commercial centres.

Historical role

Toyotomi Hideyoshi made Osaka a power base through Osaka Castle. Under Tokugawa rule, Osaka became a major centre for rice, finance and large-scale exchange.

Why it mattered

Edo was the headquarters of the Tokugawa shogunate and the centre of political and military administration.

Historical role

Tokugawa Ieyasu based his government here. Alternate attendance brought daimyo regularly to Edo. After 1868, Edo was renamed Tokyo and became the imperial capital.

Kamakura was the seat of Japan’s first enduring shogunate and represents the rise of warrior rule.

Know Japan before you enter the shogunate

Location

Japan lies in Eastern Asia, between the Asian mainland and the Pacific Ocean. Nearby countries include China, Korea and Russia.

Seas & oceans

Important waters include the Pacific Ocean, Sea of Japan and East China Sea. The sea connected Japan to neighbours but also created natural barriers.

Mountains & volcanoes

Much of Japan is mountainous and volcanic. Flat land is limited, so fertile plains and valleys became especially valuable.

Earthquakes

Japan lies on active tectonic plate boundaries in the Pacific Ring of Fire. Earthquakes, volcanoes and tsunamis are recurring natural hazards.

Food & farming

Wet-rice agriculture became central. Millet, wheat and barley were also grown. Fish and seafood provided important protein, while rice was often too valuable for poorer peasants to eat regularly.

Important cities

Nara, Kyoto, Kamakura, Edo/Tokyo, Osaka and Nagasaki all became important at different times for government, trade, religion or military rule.

Japanese legend One traditional story says the islands were formed when tears shed by a goddess fell into the sea. This is a legend, not a geological explanation.

Chapter 2 • Before the Shoguns

Japan borrowed ideas — then made them Japanese.

Before shogunal rule, contact with China and Korea transformed Japan through writing, Buddhism, Confucian ideas and models of centralised government. Nara was planned using the Chinese capital Chang’an as a model, while Kyoto later became the imperial centre.

Writing & government
Chinese characters were adapted for Japanese writing. Chinese ideas of centralised government and Confucian harmony also influenced Japan.
Nara → Kyoto
Nara became a capital in 710. Kyoto became the imperial capital in 794 and remained central to the imperial court for centuries.
Religion & values
Shinto beliefs in kami predated Buddhism. Buddhism arrived through Korea after spreading from India through China and Korea.

From early Japan to Nara and Heian

Chinese and Korean influence
Contact increased from the third and fourth centuries. Chinese characters were first used for written records, and over time they were adapted to represent Japanese sounds and ideas. Buddhism reached Japan through Korea in 552 CE after travelling from India through China and Korea. Confucian ideas about relationships between rulers and the ruled also influenced government.
Nara period — 710–794
Prince Shotoku had earlier sent ambassadors to China and promoted ideas of centralised government and Confucian harmony. Nara became the capital in 710 and was planned on a rectangular grid inspired by the Chinese capital Chang’an. Great Buddhist temples were built and Buddhism became closely linked with state power.
Heian period — 794–1156/1185
The imperial capital moved to Kyoto, then called Heian-kyo — “Capital of Peace and Ease”. Kyoto had strong transport links by river and lake. Toward the end of the period, rival noble families relied increasingly on warlords and armed retainers. This helped regional warriors gain political power.

Chapter 3 • Rise of Warrior Rule

When imperial power weakened, warriors moved into government.

Daimyo built private power through land, taxes and armed retainers. Minamoto no Yoritomo emerged from decades of conflict and was recognised as shogun. He ruled from Kamakura while the emperor kept a symbolic position in Kyoto.

Kamakura: the first warrior government

As the emperor’s political power weakened, daimyo gained wealth from private estates and taxes. Their armed protectors were called bushi or samurai. After decades of fighting, Minamoto no Yoritomo became the dominant warlord.

1185–1333

The Kamakura bakufu became Japan’s first long-lasting warrior government.

Shogun

Yoritomo accepted the title seii tai-shogun while allowing the emperor to remain as a symbolic source of legitimacy.

Bakufu

Literally connected with a military camp or tent; it came to mean government by the shogun and his warrior administration.

The shogun could appoint provincial administrators, land stewards who collected taxes, and military protectors. Yoritomo ruled from Kamakura, south of modern Tokyo, while the emperor remained in Kyoto.

Chapter 4 • Crisis at Sea

1274 and 1281: Mongol invasions test the warrior government.

Kublai Khan's forces attacked Kyushu twice. The Japanese prepared for the second invasion by building defences at Hakata Bay and training for group combat. A great storm helped end the 1281 invasion; the Japanese called it kamikaze, “divine wind”.

After the invasions: Muromachi / Ashikaga rule

The Mongol defeats did not bring lasting peace. In 1338 Ashikaga Takauji established a new warrior government in Kyoto. The period is called the Muromachi or Ashikaga shogunate. Regional daimyo grew increasingly independent, and from the fifteenth century Japan entered a long age of civil war often called the Warring States period. European traders began arriving in the sixteenth century, bringing new goods and firearms.

Chapter 5 • Unification

From divided domains to Tokugawa rule.

During the Warring States period, Japan was divided among competing daimyo. Unification was not one event: Oda Nobunaga broke old power structures, Toyotomi Hideyoshi extended control across most of Japan, and Tokugawa Ieyasu converted military victory into a durable shogunate.

Tokugawa Ieyasu — how he turned victory into a dynasty

Early life during civil war
Ieyasu was born Matsudaira Takechiyo in 1542, the son of the lord of Mikawa. As a child he was used as a hostage in alliance politics and was raised at the Imagawa court. His early life shows how insecure daimyo politics could be during the Warring States period.
Alliances and territory
Ieyasu allied with powerful leaders, including Oda Nobunaga and later Toyotomi Hideyoshi. He gained military experience and territory. Under Hideyoshi he was moved to eastern lands and made Edo his headquarters — a decision that later helped shape the Tokugawa political centre.
Sekigahara → shogun
After Hideyoshi’s death, competing daimyo coalitions formed. Ieyasu’s victory at Sekigahara in 1600 made him the strongest military leader. In 1603 the emperor granted him the title of shogun. He transferred the title to his son Hidetada in 1605, signalling that Tokugawa rule would continue as a family dynasty.
Why the Tokugawa lasted
The new regime did more than win battles. It controlled daimyo through rules on castles, marriages, military strength and movement. Alternate residence in Edo later forced daimyo to spend heavily and kept them under shogunal observation. The system preserved domains while limiting the chance that regional lords could challenge the centre.

Chapter 6 • Feudalism & Society

Do not confuse political power with social status.

Tokugawa Japan had overlapping hierarchies. The emperor remained the sacred imperial figure, the shogun exercised the strongest political and military authority, daimyo ruled domains, and samurai served the warrior elite. At the same time, the official social model ranked warriors, peasants, artisans and merchants by the kind of work they performed.

Reading the feudalism diagram

This diagram is useful because it shows the two ideas students often mix up:

How Japanese feudalism worked

Feudal relationships linked land and authority with loyalty and service. The shogun depended on daimyo to govern regions and provide military support. Daimyo depended on samurai retainers. The agricultural economy depended heavily on peasants, whose crops and taxes supported the warrior elite.

Chapter 7 • Family Life & Women

Daily life depended heavily on class, gender and family.

Life expectancy was low, disease was common, childbirth was dangerous and many children died young. Marriage was often arranged between families, and education varied strongly by wealth and gender.

Home and status: Wealthy warrior households lived very differently from farming families. Class shaped housing, clothing, literacy and opportunity.

Life inside the household

1
Health

Diseases such as tuberculosis and beri-beri were common. Women faced the added danger of childbirth.

2
Education

Girls from wealthy families might learn kana by copying poetry and practising calligraphy. Boys were more likely to study Chinese characters. Most ordinary people were illiterate.

3
Marriage

Marriages were commonly arranged between families. Household customs changed over time, including whether husbands lived with a wife’s family or wives moved into a husband’s family.

4
Beauty and clothing

Elite women could use white face powder, blacken teeth, reshape eyebrows and wear layered silk garments. Farming families wore much simpler coarse clothing.

Chapter 8 • Samurai & Culture

A warrior was expected to know more than fighting.

Samurai training included horsemanship, archery, sword fighting and unarmed combat, but also education and cultural skills. Daimyo and samurai supported poetry, Noh drama, Zen practice, tea ceremony and other arts. Tokugawa urban life also saw Kabuki grow.

Warrior culture was also artistic culture

Noh drama

Actors wore rich costumes and often masks, chanted lines and moved slowly to music. Stories were often linked to Buddhist themes and religious festivals.

Kabuki

Kabuki began around 1603. It was first associated with female performers; later men played both male and female roles. It mixed dance, music and dramatic storytelling.

Sumo

Sumo has ancient roots and was linked to Shinto ritual. Wrestlers use body weight, balance and force to push an opponent out or down.

Tea ceremony

Tea came from China. Monks used it to stay alert during meditation, and the preparation of tea developed into a slow, careful ritual emphasising discipline and attention.

Poetry

Poetry was highly valued. Matsuo Basho became famous for haiku, a short form traditionally built around 17 sound units and often focused on nature.

Zen & learning

Daimyo and samurai could support Zen monks, build temples, practise meditation, write poetry and support schools and performers.

Making a samurai

Training began early and could include horse riding, archery, sword fighting and unarmed combat. Samurai also needed education so they could help govern a domain. Cultural study could include poetry, tea ceremony and flower arranging.

Bushido emphasised discipline, honour, duty and loyalty to one’s lord and family. Samurai idealised readiness to die rather than accept disgrace. Seppuku was ritual suicide associated with this honour culture; it is a historical practice and is not acceptable or appropriate today.

Chapter 9 • How Tokugawa Control Worked

Control the daimyo, control the realm.

Tokugawa rule was not maintained by one policy. Tokugawa rule used castle restrictions, limits on daimyo alliances and military resources, alternate residence in Edo, regulation of movement and land use, and later tighter controls on foreign contact.

How the Tokugawa kept powerful lords under control

  • Tokugawa shoguns directly controlled about a quarter of Japan, while hundreds of daimyo ruled the remaining domains.
  • Daimyo could not marry into alliances without shogunal approval.
  • They could not freely build new castles, and major repairs needed approval.
  • The number of samurai a daimyo could maintain could be limited.
  • Alternate residence required daimyo to spend regular periods in Edo. Travel and maintaining residences were expensive and reduced their ability to organise rebellion.
  • Trusted lords were placed in strategic lands, while former enemies or tozama (“outer lords”) were often placed farther from the centre and watched closely.

Forests, timber and power

Wood was essential for houses, castles, ships, heating and industry. Clearing forests could create new farmland, increasing a daimyo’s wealth, peasant population and ability to support samurai. Tokugawa rulers therefore treated forest use as both an environmental and political issue.

Rapid town growth caused serious deforestation, erosion, landslides and floods. In 1666 the use of wood required authorisation from a shogun or daimyo. Forestry experiments included protecting seedlings, selective cutting and later plantations. By the eighteenth century, Japan had developed detailed knowledge of plantation forestry and community forest management.

Chapter 10 • Foreign Contact

“Closed Japan” is too simple.

Restrictions tightened in the 1630s: Christianity was banned, Japanese overseas travel was prohibited, Portuguese ships were excluded and other foreign trade was tightly regulated. But contact did not disappear. Dutch and Chinese trade continued through Nagasaki/Dejima, and contact with Korea also continued.

Dejima, Nagasaki. Controlled contact with Dutch traders helped Japan continue to receive selected foreign goods, books and scientific knowledge.

Myth or fact?

“Tokugawa Japan had zero contact with outsiders.”

“Christianity was treated as a political and religious threat by the shogunate.”

“Dutch books contributed to knowledge of Western science.”

What “sakoku” actually meant

European contact began with Portuguese traders in 1543 and the missionary Francis Xavier in 1549. New crops, navigation knowledge and shipbuilding ideas appeared, but firearms attracted particular attention and were copied rapidly.

Restrictions, 1633–1639
Tokugawa edicts banned Christianity, prohibited Japanese from travelling abroad, excluded Portuguese ships and tightly regulated other foreign trade. The government feared Christianity as a rival source of authority and feared that outer daimyo might grow rich through overseas trade.
Contact that continued
Dutch traders were confined to Dejima in Nagasaki and focused on commerce rather than missionary work. Chinese traders also continued, and Korea remained an important diplomatic contact. Dutch books helped some Japanese scholars learn about Western medicine, astronomy and science, while political study was restricted.
Positive and negative effects
Some merchants became wealthy from permitted trade and the shogun strengthened political control. However, technological exchange narrowed, shipbuilders and sailors lost opportunities, and during famines Japan could not easily import food or seed. People interested in Western ideas could face punishment.
Peasant rebellions
Tokugawa controls caused resentment. Peasants, merchants and craftspeople could join uprisings when taxes, famine or restrictions became unbearable. More than 2000 rebellions occurred against Tokugawa rule. Some were linked to religion, including a major Christian uprising around 1637.

Chapter 11 • The Shogunate Falls

Perry, unequal pressure, internal opposition — and 1868.

By the nineteenth century the Tokugawa government faced “troubles from within and without”. Commodore Matthew Perry arrived in 1853, and the Kanagawa Treaty followed in 1854. Opposition grew among powerful domains. Tokugawa rule ended in 1867–1868 and political authority returned to the emperor in the Meiji Restoration.

The political order changed dramatically after 1868. Modernisation then transformed transport, military organisation, education and industry.

Why Tokugawa rule collapsed

By the early nineteenth century, class roles were shifting. Samurai often worked as bureaucrats and struggled with rising living costs, while merchants — officially low in status — could become wealthy. Crop failures still caused famine and violence.

Perry’s demands

On 8 July 1853 Commodore Matthew Perry arrived in Edo Bay with four US naval ships. The United States wanted trade, humane treatment of shipwrecked sailors, and ports where steamships could take coal, food and water. Perry returned in 1854 with a larger force.

Kanagawa Treaty, 1854

The shogunate agreed that American ships could use two ports, shipwrecked sailors would be treated well, and an American consul could reside in Japan. Russia, France, Britain and the Netherlands soon gained similar agreements. Later treaties also gave foreigners legal privileges on Japanese soil, causing anger and humiliation.

Opposition grows

Reformist domains learned Western military and industrial technology. Choshu and Satsuma formed an alliance in 1866. Tokugawa Yoshinobu resigned in 1867, and in January 1868 the office of shogun was abolished. The young Emperor Meiji became the centre of the restored imperial government, and Edo was renamed Tokyo.

Chapter 12 • Meiji Restoration & Modernisation

To resist Western control, Japan transformed itself.

Many supporters of imperial restoration originally wanted to remove foreign influence. Instead, the new government concluded that Japan had to learn from Western technology and institutions in order to remain independent and powerful.

What changed?

1
Government & class

The feudal class system was abolished and daimyo power was reduced. Former samurai lost many old privileges, including the right to wear swords.

2
Military

A national army was created using modern guns and artillery. The army learned from Prussia; the navy drew on British and Dutch models.

3
Education

Primary education became compulsory for boys and girls. Universities were created and foreign teachers were invited.

4
Industry & transport

Factories, railways, telegraph networks and postal services were developed. Coal production increased to power steamships and railroads.

Emperor Meiji photographed in 1873. The Meiji era became associated with rapid political, military, educational and industrial transformation.

Why modernise?

A Japanese mission of about fifty experts travelled abroad to study schools, hospitals, factories and government systems. Leaders believed modernisation was necessary to avoid the fate of countries that had fallen under Western control. Japanese students travelled overseas, foreign engineers and teachers came to Japan, and Western clothing, music and habits became more visible.

Modernisation did not simply erase Japanese traditions. Former samurai, already educated and politically experienced, filled many roles in the new administration. Japan repeatedly borrowed foreign ideas and adapted them to local needs.
